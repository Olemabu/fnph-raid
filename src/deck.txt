## Main Gate | Definitions & psychopathology
> Psychopathology is the language of psychiatry. Descriptive psychopathology (Karl Jaspers) separates FORM from CONTENT.
> - Form = the type of experience (a hallucination, a delusion, an obsession).
> - Content = what it is about (voices about witchcraft, a belief of being poisoned).
> Exams reward naming the form precisely. Sort every phenomenon into its family: perception (hallucination, illusion, pseudohallucination), thought content (delusion, overvalued idea, obsession), self-experience (depersonalisation, derealisation, passivity) and memory (confabulation).

Q: Delusion = A fixed, false belief held despite contrary evidence and out of keeping with the person's culture | A fixed, false belief held despite contrary evidence, even when shared by the person's culture :: Culturally shared beliefs are excluded; a delusion must be out of keeping with the person's culture | A false belief held with some doubt that yields to evidence :: A belief that yields to evidence is a mistake or an overvalued idea; delusions are held with full conviction | A fixed, false belief the person recognises as senseless and resists :: Recognising it as senseless and resisting it describes an obsession
> Jaspers' criteria: held with extraordinary conviction, not changed by counter-argument or experience, and false or impossible in content.
> - Culture matters: beliefs shared by the person's culture (for example witchcraft or spiritual attack in many Nigerian communities) are NOT delusions.
> - Primary (autochthonous) delusions arise suddenly with no explanation. Secondary delusions are understandable from another experience such as low mood or hallucinations.
> - Classify by content: persecutory, grandiose, nihilistic, jealous, erotomanic, somatic, of reference, of guilt.

Q: Hallucination = A perception without an external stimulus, with the full quality of a real perception | A perception without an external stimulus, located in inner subjective space :: Inner space and reduced vividness describe a pseudohallucination | A perception of a real external stimulus, distorted by mood or expectation :: A real stimulus distorted by mood is an (affect) illusion | A perception without an external stimulus that the person can summon and dismiss at will :: Voluntary control describes mental imagery; hallucinations are involuntary
> A true hallucination is vivid and substantial, located in external space, and not under voluntary control.
> - Describe it by modality: auditory (commonest in schizophrenia), visual (think organic causes and delirium), tactile, olfactory, gustatory, somatic.
> - Hypnagogic (falling asleep) and hypnopompic (waking up) hallucinations can be normal.
> - Third-person voices arguing or commenting are first-rank symptoms.

Q: Illusion = A misperception of a real external stimulus | A perception without any external stimulus, with full vividness :: No stimulus at all makes it a hallucination | A normal perception of a real stimulus given a sudden delusional meaning :: That is delusional perception; the perception itself is normal | A misperception of a real stimulus held afterwards with unshakeable conviction :: Once it is held with fixed conviction it has become a delusion
> Three types of illusion:
> - Completion illusions: the mind fills gaps, like reading a word with a missing letter.
> - Affect illusions: mood shapes perception, like a frightened person seeing a shadow as an attacker.
> - Pareidolia: vivid images seen in vague stimuli, like faces in clouds. It persists even with attention.
> Illusions increase with fatigue, fever, poor lighting and delirium.

Q: Pseudohallucination = A perception experienced in inner subjective space, lacking full vividness | A perception in external space with full vividness, recognised as unreal :: External space and full vividness make it a true hallucination, even with insight | A misperception of a real stimulus, located in inner space :: A misperception of a real stimulus is an illusion | A vivid mental image fully under voluntary control :: Voluntary control describes imagery; pseudohallucinations are involuntary
> The usual teaching (Kandinsky, Jaspers): it lacks the substantial quality of real perception and is located in inner subjective space ("in my head").
> - The person may recognise that it is not real.
> - Seen in grief, PTSD, dissociative states and personality disorders, as well as psychosis.
> - Definitions vary between textbooks. State the definition you are using.

Q: Overvalued idea = A plausible, emotionally charged belief that dominates life but is not delusional | A plausible belief that dominates life, held with full delusional conviction :: Full delusional conviction makes it a delusion | A plausible, emotionally charged idea that the person resists as senseless :: Resisting it as senseless marks an obsession; overvalued ideas are embraced | An implausible belief that dominates life but is not delusional :: Overvalued ideas are understandable and plausible, not implausible
> Described by Wernicke. The belief is understandable given the person's personality and history, but it dominates their life and actions.
> - Examples: body-shape beliefs in anorexia nervosa, non-delusional morbid jealousy, hypochondriacal preoccupation.
> - Unlike an obsession, it is NOT resisted: the person agrees with it (ego-syntonic).

Q: Obsession = A recurrent intrusive thought, recognised as one's own and usually resisted | A recurrent intrusive thought felt to be placed in the mind by an outside force :: Thoughts felt to come from outside are thought insertion, a first-rank symptom | A recurrent thought recognised as one's own that the person welcomes :: Obsessions are unwanted and distressing (ego-dystonic) | A recurrent intrusive image recognised as coming from outside the mind :: Obsessions are recognised as products of one's OWN mind
> The four features of an obsession: intrusive, recognised as a product of one's own mind, unwanted and distressing (ego-dystonic), and usually resisted.
> - Compulsions are repetitive acts or mental rituals done to reduce the anxiety.
> - ICD-11 OCD specifies insight: good or fair, poor, or absent. Poor insight does not make it psychosis.

Q: Delusional perception = A normal perception given a sudden delusional meaning | A false perception that is given a delusional meaning :: In delusional perception the perception itself is normal | A normal perception interpreted in line with an existing delusion :: That is delusional misinterpretation; delusional perception arises suddenly, not from an existing delusion | A normal perception that triggers a vivid, upsetting memory :: That is ordinary association, not a delusion
> A Schneiderian first-rank symptom with two stages: a real, normal perception, then a sudden delusional meaning with no logical link.
> - Example: "The traffic light turned red, and I knew I had been chosen to save Nigeria."
> - Often preceded by delusional mood: a sense that something strange and important is about to happen.

Q: Depersonalisation = Feeling that oneself is unreal or detached | Feeling that the surroundings are unreal or flat :: That is derealisation | A fixed belief that one does not exist :: With fixed conviction it is a nihilistic (Cotard) delusion; depersonalisation keeps insight | Feeling that one's actions are controlled by an outside force :: That is a passivity experience
> The person feels unreal, detached, or like an observer of themselves. They describe it "as if", and insight is kept.
> - Seen in anxiety, depression, exhaustion, temporal lobe epilepsy and depersonalisation-derealisation disorder.
> - If insight is lost ("I AM dead"), it becomes a nihilistic delusion.

Q: Derealisation = Feeling that the surroundings are unreal | Feeling that oneself is unreal or detached :: That is depersonalisation | A fixed belief that the world is no longer real :: With fixed conviction it is a nihilistic delusion; derealisation is 'as if', with insight | Seeing objects as smaller than they really are :: That is micropsia, a perceptual distortion
> The world feels flat, distant, dreamlike, or like a film set. It often occurs with depersonalisation, and insight is preserved.

Q: Confabulation = Filling memory gaps with false memories, without intent to deceive | Filling memory gaps with false memories, deliberately to deceive :: Confabulation has no intent to deceive; the patient believes it | Recalling a real memory with a new delusional meaning :: That is a delusional memory | Recalling false memories in an alert patient with intact memory :: Confabulation arises from memory impairment, as in Korsakoff syndrome
> "Honest lying." The classic setting is Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine deficiency, usually from alcohol); it is also seen with frontal lobe damage.
> - Provoked confabulation appears in response to questions. Spontaneous (fantastic) confabulation is rarer and suggests frontal involvement.

Q: Alexithymia = Difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions | Difficulty reading written words :: That is alexia | Difficulty recognising emotions in other people's faces :: Alexithymia is about recognising and naming one's OWN emotions | Inability to experience pleasure :: That is anhedonia
> Term by Sifneos. Greek: a (without) + lexis (word) + thymos (emotion).
> - Linked to somatic symptom disorders, autism, PTSD and substance use. These people often report bodily sensations instead of feelings.

Q: Anhedonia = Loss of interest or pleasure in usual activities | Reduced outward expression of emotion :: Reduced expression is blunted or flattened affect, which the examiner observes | Lack of motivation to start activities :: That is avolition | Inability to put feelings into words :: That is alexithymia
> One of the two AFFECTIVE-cluster symptoms of an ICD-11 depressive episode (with depressed mood), so it can satisfy the core requirement on its own.
> - It is also a negative symptom of schizophrenia.

Q: Echolalia = Repeating another person's words | Repeating one's own words or phrases :: That is palilalia | Imitating another person's movements :: That is echopraxia | Repeating the last syllable of one's own words :: That is logoclonia
> Echo phenomena are echolalia (words) and echopraxia (movements).
> - Seen in catatonia, autism, Tourette syndrome and frontal lobe disease.
> - Do not confuse with palilalia (repeating your OWN words) or logoclonia (repeating the last syllable).

Q: Solastalgia = Distress caused by environmental change to one's home place | Anxiety about future climate change :: That is eco-anxiety, which looks ahead | Grief for ecosystems that have already been lost :: That is ecological grief | Homesickness after moving away from home :: That is nostalgia; solastalgia happens while you are still at home
> Coined by philosopher Glenn Albrecht (2005) from solace + nostalgia + algia (pain): the pain of losing the comfort of home while still living there.
> - Triggers: mining, drought, flooding, deforestation, desertification (relevant to northern Nigeria).
> - Related terms: eco-anxiety (future-focused worry) and ecological grief (mourning lost ecosystems).

Q: Insight = Awareness of being ill, of its cause, and of the need for treatment | Awareness of being ill, without needing to accept treatment :: Accepting the need for treatment is one of the three parts of insight | Agreement with every part of the doctor's view :: Insight is graded and multidimensional, not simple agreement | The ability to make sound decisions in daily life :: That is judgement
> Insight is graded, not all-or-nothing. David (1990) described three dimensions: recognising you have an illness, relabelling unusual experiences as part of it, and accepting treatment.
> Record it in the MSE in the patient's own words.

L: Piaget's stages of cognitive development = Sensorimotor | Preoperational | Concrete operational | Formal operational ~~ Latency :: That is a Freudian stage | Industry vs inferiority :: That is an Erikson stage | Phallic :: That is a Freudian stage
> - Sensorimotor (0-2 years): learns through senses and action; object permanence develops.
> - Preoperational (2-7): symbolic play; egocentrism and animism; cannot yet conserve.
> - Concrete operational (7-11): conservation and logical thinking about concrete things.
> - Formal operational (11+): abstract and hypothetical reasoning.

L: Freud's psychosexual stages = Oral | Anal | Phallic | Latency | Genital ~~ Sensorimotor :: That is Piaget's first stage | Concrete operational :: That is a Piaget stage | Trust vs mistrust :: That is Erikson's first stage
> Oral (0-1), anal (1-3), phallic (3-6, Oedipus complex), latency (6 to puberty), genital (puberty onwards).
> Fixation at a stage is said to shape personality. For example, anal traits are orderliness, obstinacy and parsimony.

L: Erikson's psychosocial stages = Trust vs mistrust | Autonomy vs shame and doubt | Initiative vs guilt | Industry vs inferiority | Identity vs role confusion | Intimacy vs isolation | Generativity vs stagnation | Integrity vs despair ~~ Oral :: That is a Freudian stage | Formal operational :: That is a Piaget stage | Hope vs fear :: Not one of Erikson's stages
> Eight stages across the whole lifespan. Each is a crisis that, if resolved, gives a virtue:
> - Infancy, trust: hope. Early childhood, autonomy: will. Preschool, initiative: purpose. School age, industry: competence.
> - Adolescence, identity: fidelity. Young adult, intimacy: love. Middle age, generativity: care. Old age, integrity: wisdom.

## Car Park | Acronyms & screening tools
> Screening tools pick out who needs a full assessment. They do NOT make a diagnosis.
> For every tool, know: what the letters stand for, what it measures, how many items, the maximum score and the usual cut-offs.

Q: What does CAGE screen for? = Problem drinking (alcohol use disorder) | Severity of alcohol withdrawal :: CIWA-Ar rates withdrawal severity | Hazardous drinking in people who are not dependent :: CAGE misses many hazardous, non-dependent drinkers; AUDIT is better for that | Misuse of drugs other than alcohol :: CAGE-AID adapts it for drugs; CAGE itself is about alcohol
> CAGE (Ewing, 1984): four yes/no questions.
> - C: Have you ever felt you should CUT DOWN on your drinking?
> - A: Have people ANNOYED you by criticising your drinking?
> - G: Have you ever felt bad or GUILTY about your drinking?
> - E: Have you ever had a drink first thing in the morning to steady your nerves or get rid of a hangover (EYE-OPENER)?
> Two or more "yes" answers is clinically significant. CAGE is quick but misses hazardous, non-dependent drinking; AUDIT is better for that.

L: CAGE = Cut down | Annoyed | Guilty | Eye-opener ~~ Craving :: Not in CAGE. C is Cut down | Anxious :: Not in CAGE. A is Annoyed (by criticism of drinking) | Gambling :: Not in CAGE. G is Guilty | Early-morning waking :: Not in CAGE. E is Eye-opener (a morning drink)
> The E is the most specific item: a morning drink to treat withdrawal points towards dependence.

Q: How many CAGE "yes" answers are clinically significant? = 2 or more | 1 :: One "yes" deserves more questions, but the usual threshold is 2 | 3 or more :: Too high; 2 or more is significant | All 4 :: Too high; 2 or more is significant
> Two or more "yes" answers suggests an alcohol use disorder and needs a full alcohol history.

Q: What does AUDIT stand for? = Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test | Alcohol Use Disorders Inventory Test :: The I stands for Identification | Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Tool :: The T stands for Test | Alcohol Use and Drinking Identification Test :: It is Alcohol Use DISORDERS Identification Test
> AUDIT was developed by the WHO for use across cultures and in primary care.

Q: AUDIT screens for = Hazardous and harmful alcohol use | Alcohol dependence only :: AUDIT picks up hazardous and harmful use, not only dependence | Severity of alcohol withdrawal :: CIWA-Ar rates withdrawal severity | Lifetime alcohol exposure in units :: AUDIT asks mainly about the past year, and scores patterns and harm, not lifetime units
> AUDIT has 10 questions, each scored 0-4, for a maximum of 40.
> - Q1-3: consumption. Q4-6: dependence symptoms. Q7-10: alcohol-related harm.
> - Zones: 0-7 low risk. 8-15 hazardous. 16-19 harmful. 20 or more: possible dependence, so refer.
> - AUDIT-C is the short version, using only the first three (consumption) questions.

L: AUDIT sections in question order = Consumption (Q1-3) | Dependence symptoms (Q4-6) | Alcohol-related harm (Q7-10) ~~ Withdrawal seizures (Q11-12) :: AUDIT has only 10 questions | Family history (Q1-2) :: Not part of AUDIT | Craving scale :: Not an AUDIT section
> Remember the order: how much you drink, whether you are hooked, and what harm it is causing.

Q: AUDIT score suggesting possible dependence = 20 or more | 8 or more :: 8-15 is the hazardous zone | 16 or more :: 16-19 is the harmful zone | 40 :: 40 is the maximum score
> Zones: 0-7 low risk, 8-15 hazardous, 16-19 harmful, 20+ possible dependence.

Q: PHQ-9 screens for = Depression | Depression in older adults specifically :: That is the Geriatric Depression Scale | Postnatal depression specifically :: That is the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) | Generalised anxiety :: GAD-7 screens for generalised anxiety
> Patient Health Questionnaire-9: nine items that mirror the DSM depression criteria. Each is scored 0-3 for the last 2 weeks, so the maximum is 27.
> - Severity: 5 mild, 10 moderate, 15 moderately severe, 20 severe.
> - Item 9 asks about thoughts of death or self-harm. ANY positive answer needs a risk assessment.
> - PHQ-2 is the first two items (low mood and loss of interest), a quick pre-screen.

Q: PHQ-9 cut-off usually used for moderate depression = 10 | 5 :: 5 marks mild | 15 :: 15 marks moderately severe | 20 :: 20 marks severe
> 5, 10, 15, 20: mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe.

Q: GAD-7 screens for = Generalised anxiety | Depression :: PHQ-9 screens for depression | Panic disorder only :: It was built for GAD, and also picks up panic, social anxiety and PTSD | Health anxiety :: Not its purpose
> Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7: seven items scored 0-3 over 2 weeks, so the maximum is 21.
> - Cut-offs: 5 mild, 10 moderate, 15 severe. A score of 10 or more is a common referral threshold.
> - It also picks up panic disorder, social anxiety and PTSD reasonably well.

Q: Maximum MMSE score = 30 | 28 :: The MMSE is scored out of 30 | 26 :: 26 is the usual MoCA cut-off, not the MMSE maximum | 24 :: 24 is a commonly used MMSE cut-off, not the maximum
> Mini-Mental State Examination (Folstein, 1975):
> - Orientation 10 (time 5, place 5). Registration 3. Attention and calculation 5 (serial 7s or WORLD backwards). Recall 3.
> - Language and praxis 9: naming 2, repetition 1, three-stage command 3, reading 1, writing 1, copying pentagons 1.
> - 23 or below commonly suggests impairment; adjust for education and language. It is weak on executive function, where the MoCA is more sensitive for mild impairment.

S: MMSE sections = Orientation (10) | Registration (3) | Attention and calculation (5) | Recall (3) | Language and praxis (9) ~~ Abstract reasoning :: Not an MMSE section (the MoCA tests abstraction) | Insight :: Not a cognitive test item | Mood :: Not part of the MMSE
> 10 + 3 + 5 + 3 + 9 = 30.

Q: What does CIWA-Ar stand for? = Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised | Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Anxiety, revised :: The A is for Alcohol | Clinical Inventory of Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, rapid :: It is Institute, and r means revised | Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Addiction, rated :: It is Alcohol, revised
> The "-Ar" means Alcohol, revised.

Q: CIWA-Ar measures = Severity of alcohol withdrawal | Severity of opioid withdrawal :: Opioid withdrawal is rated with COWS | Risk of developing alcohol withdrawal later :: CIWA-Ar rates the signs and symptoms present NOW | Severity of alcohol dependence :: Dependence is judged from the ICD-11 features (or SADQ); CIWA-Ar rates withdrawal
> CIWA-Ar has 10 items: nausea and vomiting, tremor, paroxysmal sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile disturbances, auditory disturbances, visual disturbances, headache, and orientation (clouding of sensorium). Maximum 67.
> Commonly: under about 8-10 mild, 8-15 moderate, over 15 severe (higher risk of seizures and delirium tremens). It guides symptom-triggered benzodiazepine dosing.

Q: What does COWS stand for? = Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale | Clinical Opioid Withdrawal Score :: It is Opiate, and S is Scale | Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Survey :: S is Scale | Comprehensive Opiate Withdrawal Scale :: C is Clinical
> COWS rates opioid withdrawal signs: pulse, sweating, restlessness, pupil size, aches, runny nose and tearing, GI upset, tremor, yawning, anxiety and gooseflesh. It is the opioid counterpart of CIWA-Ar.

L: SAD PERSONS = Sex (male) | Age (under 19 or over 45) | Depression | Previous attempt | Ethanol use | Rational thinking loss | Social support lacking | Organised plan | No spouse | Sickness ~~ Female sex :: Male sex is the risk factor (S) | Employed :: Not in SAD PERSONS | Good social support :: Lacking social support is the risk factor (S) | Married :: No spouse is the risk factor (N)
> Patterson et al. (1983) suicide-risk mnemonic; each item scores 1 point.
> The original guide was 0-2 low, 3-4 close follow-up, 5-6 consider admission, 7-10 admit.
> Modern guidance (for example NICE) warns AGAINST using risk scales to predict suicide or decide care. Use it as a memory aid for a full clinical risk assessment.

L: MSE order (ASEPTIC) = Appearance and behaviour | Speech | Emotion (mood and affect) | Perception | Thought (form and content) | Insight and judgement | Cognition ~~ Past psychiatric history :: That is part of the history, not the MSE | Family history :: That is part of the history, not the MSE | Physical examination :: Separate from the MSE | Diagnosis :: The diagnosis comes after the assessment, not inside the MSE
> - Appearance and behaviour: dress, hygiene, eye contact, rapport, psychomotor activity.
> - Speech: rate, volume, tone, quantity.
> - Emotion: mood (subjective, in the patient's words) and affect (observed: range, reactivity, congruence).
> - Perception: hallucinations, illusions.
> - Thought: form (flight of ideas, loosening of associations) and content (delusions, obsessions, suicidal or homicidal ideas).
> - Insight and judgement, then cognition (orientation, attention, memory).
> Many formats put thought before perception. Use the order your centre expects.

L: SIG E CAPS = Sleep | Interest | Guilt | Energy | Concentration | Appetite | Psychomotor change | Suicidality ~~ Elevated mood :: That is a manic symptom | Pressured speech :: That is a manic symptom | Grandiosity :: That is a manic symptom | Hallucinations :: Not part of SIG E CAPS
> A memory hook ("prescribe SIG: Energy CAPSules") for the depressive symptoms other than low mood.
> DSM-5 needs 5 of 9: depressed mood or loss of interest, plus the rest from this list. ICD-11 uses 10 symptoms, adding hopelessness.

L: HPC five Cs (FNPH long case) = Complaint | Course | Cause (patient's perspective) | Complications | Care so far ~~ Collateral history :: A separate section, not one of the five Cs | Cognition :: That belongs to the mental state examination | Comorbidity :: Not one of the five Cs
> FNPH style: write each presenting complaint as its own paragraph (complaint, course, cause from the patient's perspective, phenomenology).
> Complications and care so far come ONCE, at the bottom, after all the symptoms.

L: SPIKES (breaking bad news) = Setting up | Perception | Invitation | Knowledge | Emotions (respond with empathy) | Strategy and summary ~~ Silence :: Not a SPIKES step | Prognosis first :: First check what the person already knows (Perception) | Paperwork :: Not a SPIKES step
> Baile and Buckman (2000):
> - Setting: privacy, sit down, involve family if wanted.
> - Perception: what do they already know?
> - Invitation: how much do they want to know?
> - Knowledge: give a warning shot, use plain words, give small chunks.
> - Emotions: name the feeling and respond with empathy.
> - Strategy and summary: agree the plan and check understanding.

L: Stages of change (Prochaska and DiClemente) = Precontemplation | Contemplation | Preparation | Action | Maintenance ~~ Denial :: That is a Kubler-Ross grief stage | Bargaining :: That is a Kubler-Ross grief stage | Acceptance :: That is a Kubler-Ross grief stage
> The transtheoretical model (relapse can happen at any point). Match the intervention to the stage:
> - Precontemplation: raise doubt and give information.
> - Contemplation: explore ambivalence (decisional balance).
> - Preparation: make a plan. Action: support the change. Maintenance: relapse prevention.
> This is the backbone of motivational interviewing.

## Main Block | ICD-11 depressive episode
> ICD-11 depressive episode: at least 5 of 10 symptoms, most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks. At least ONE must come from the affective cluster.
> - Affective (2): depressed mood; markedly diminished interest or pleasure.
> - Cognitive-behavioural (4): reduced concentration; worthlessness or excessive guilt; hopelessness; recurrent thoughts of death or suicide.
> - Neurovegetative (4): sleep disturbance; appetite or weight change; psychomotor agitation or retardation; reduced energy.
> Grade it mild, moderate or severe, with or without psychotic symptoms. A past manic or hypomanic episode makes it bipolar instead.
> Codes: 6A70 single episode, 6A71 recurrent, 6A72 dysthymic, 6A73 mixed depressive and anxiety disorder.

Q: Minimum duration of a depressive episode (ICD-11) = At least 2 weeks | At least 1 week :: One week is the usual minimum for a manic episode | At least 1 month :: One month is the ICD-11 duration for schizophrenia | At least 6 months :: Too long; a depressive episode needs at least 2 weeks
> Same 2-week threshold as DSM-5. Learn the duration ladder: hypomania several days, mania 1 week (less if hospitalised or treated), depressive episode 2 weeks, schizophrenia 1 month, dysthymia 2 years.

Q: Number of symptoms required for an ICD-11 depressive episode = At least 5 of 10 | At least 3 of 10 :: Too few; ICD-11 needs at least 5 of the 10 | At least 4 of 10 :: Too few; ICD-11 needs at least 5 of the 10 | At least 6 of 10 :: Too many; 5 of the 10 is enough
> ICD-11 has 10 symptoms because it adds HOPELESSNESS to the 9 DSM criteria. Both systems need 5.

Q: At least one of the symptoms must be = Depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure | Depressed mood or reduced energy :: Reduced energy is neurovegetative; the core pair is low mood and loss of interest | Diminished interest or hopelessness :: Hopelessness is cognitive-behavioural, not affective | Both depressed mood AND diminished interest :: Only ONE of the two is required
> These two make up the affective cluster. Without one of them, it is not a depressive episode, however many other symptoms there are.

Q: Which is NOT an ICD-11 depressive symptom? = Pressured speech | Hopelessness :: Hopelessness IS one of the 10 (ICD-11 added it) | Psychomotor agitation or retardation :: Agitation IS included (neurovegetative cluster) | Indecisiveness :: Indecisiveness IS included, with reduced concentration
> Pressured speech is a MANIC symptom. If manic and depressive symptoms mix within the episode, think of a mixed episode (bipolar).

Q: ICD-11 code for single episode depressive disorder = 6A70 | 6A71 :: 6A71 is recurrent depressive disorder | 6A20 :: 6A20 is schizophrenia | 6B00 :: 6B00 is generalised anxiety disorder
> The mood-disorder block: 6A60 bipolar I, 6A61 bipolar II, 6A62 cyclothymic, 6A70 single episode depressive, 6A71 recurrent depressive, 6A72 dysthymic, 6A73 mixed depressive and anxiety.

Q: ICD-11 code for recurrent depressive disorder = 6A71 | 6A70 :: 6A70 is single episode depressive disorder | 6A60 :: 6A60 is bipolar type I disorder | 6A72 :: 6A72 is dysthymic disorder
> "Recurrent" means at least two depressive episodes, separated by several months without significant mood disturbance.

Q: ICD-11 dysthymic disorder needs persistent low mood for at least = 2 years | 1 year :: 1 year is the DSM-5 rule for children and adolescents; ICD-11 uses 2 years | 6 months :: Too short for dysthymia | 3 years :: Too long; it is 2 years
> Dysthymic disorder (6A72): persistent depressive mood most of the day, more days than not, for at least 2 years, without full depressive episodes in the first 2 years.

L: ICD-11 depressive symptom clusters = Affective | Cognitive-behavioural | Neurovegetative ~~ Psychotic :: Not an ICD-11 depressive cluster | Manic :: Not an ICD-11 depressive cluster | Perceptual :: Not an ICD-11 depressive cluster
> Affective 2, cognitive-behavioural 4, neurovegetative 4 = 10 symptoms.

S: The 10 ICD-11 depressive episode symptoms = Depressed mood | Diminished interest or pleasure | Reduced concentration or indecisiveness | Low self-worth or excessive guilt | Hopelessness about the future | Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide | Disturbed sleep | Change in appetite or weight | Psychomotor agitation or retardation | Reduced energy or fatigue ~~ Pressured speech :: That is a manic symptom | Grandiosity :: That is a manic symptom | Increased goal-directed activity :: That is a manic symptom | Flight of ideas :: That is a manic symptom
> Group them as you shoot: 2 affective, 4 cognitive-behavioural, 4 neurovegetative.

L: Kubler-Ross stages of grief = Denial | Anger | Bargaining | Depression | Acceptance ~~ Contemplation :: That is a stage of change | Precontemplation :: That is a stage of change | Maintenance :: That is a stage of change
> From "On Death and Dying" (1969). People move back and forth; the stages are not strictly sequential.
> ICD-11 prolonged grief disorder (6B42): persistent longing or preoccupation with the deceased, with intense emotional pain, lasting at least 6 months after the loss and beyond cultural norms.

Q: ICD-11 prolonged grief disorder: minimum time since the loss = At least 6 months | At least 12 months :: 12 months is the DSM-5-TR rule for adults; ICD-11 uses 6 months | At least 3 months :: Too short | At least 2 years :: Too long; it is 6 months
> It must also clearly exceed what is expected in the person's social, cultural or religious context.

L: Starting an antidepressant (NICE outline) = Assess severity and suicide risk | Start an SSRI as first line | Review within 2 weeks (1 week if under 25 or at risk) | If little response at 4 weeks, adjust dose or switch | Continue for at least 6 months after remission | Taper gradually when stopping ~~ Stop abruptly when well :: Stopping suddenly risks discontinuation symptoms; taper | Start with an MAOI :: MAOIs are not first line | Review only after 3 months :: Far too late; review within 2 weeks
> Warn about early side effects, the 2-4 week delay in benefit, and possible early agitation or suicidal thoughts in young people.
> After remission, continue at least 6 months (longer if recurrent). Stop by tapering over weeks.

## NE Wing | Anxiety, OCD & trauma
> ICD-11 groups these into three blocks:
> - Anxiety or fear-related (6B0-): 6B00 GAD, 6B01 panic, 6B02 agoraphobia, 6B03 specific phobia, 6B04 social anxiety, 6B05 separation anxiety, 6B06 selective mutism.
> - Obsessive-compulsive or related (6B2-): 6B20 OCD, 6B21 body dysmorphic, 6B22 olfactory reference, 6B23 hypochondriasis, 6B24 hoarding, 6B25 body-focused repetitive behaviour.
> - Specifically associated with stress (6B4-): 6B40 PTSD, 6B41 complex PTSD, 6B42 prolonged grief, 6B43 adjustment disorder.

Q: ICD-11 code for generalised anxiety disorder = 6B00 | 6B20 :: 6B20 is OCD | 6B40 :: 6B40 is PTSD | 6A70 :: 6A70 is single episode depressive disorder
> 6B0- is the anxiety block, and GAD is its first member.

Q: ICD-11 code for obsessive-compulsive disorder = 6B20 | 6B00 :: 6B00 is GAD | 6B40 :: 6B40 is PTSD | 6B41 :: 6B41 is complex PTSD

## Pink Quad | Schizophrenia: Diagnosis & Treatment Resistance
> ICD-11 schizophrenia (6A20): at least TWO of the following, at least one from a-d, for at least 1 month:
> - a. Persistent delusions. b. Persistent hallucinations. c. Disorganised thinking. d. Experiences of influence, passivity or control.
> - e. Negative symptoms. f. Grossly disorganised behaviour. g. Psychomotor disturbances, including catatonia.
> ICD-11 dropped the old subtypes (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic). It uses symptom specifiers (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive).
> Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS): non-response to at least TWO different antipsychotics (at least one second-generation) at adequate therapeutic dose for 6–8 weeks each. TRS is the primary indication for Clozapine.

Q: Minimum duration for schizophrenia (ICD-11) = 1 month | 6 months :: Six months is the DSM-5 total duration, not ICD-11 | 2 weeks :: Two weeks is the depressive episode duration | 3 months :: Not an ICD-11 threshold; it is 1 month
> Shorter psychotic presentations fit acute and transient psychotic disorder (6A23), which starts suddenly and typically resolves within 3 months.

Q: Symptom requirement for schizophrenia (ICD-11) = At least 2 symptoms, at least one from the core group | At least 2 symptoms from any of the groups :: At least one must come from the core group | At least 1 core symptom for 1 month :: Two symptoms are needed | At least 2 core symptoms :: Only one must be core; the second can be from any group
> Core group = delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, and passivity experiences.

Q: How is Treatment-Resistant Schizophrenia (TRS) defined? = Failure of at least 2 antipsychotics (≥1 SGA) at adequate dose for 6–8 weeks each | Failure of 1 antipsychotic for 2 weeks :: 2 weeks is too short to evaluate clinical response | Failure of typical antipsychotics only :: Current guidelines require at least one trial of a second-generation atypical | High dose depot for 6 months :: Resistance requires documented non-response to two distinct trials, not prolonged depot monotherapy
> Kane (1988) and McEvoy (2006): TRS requires failure of at least two antipsychotics at therapeutic doses for at least 6–8 weeks. It affects ~30% of patients and warrants Clozapine evaluation.

S: ICD-11 schizophrenia core symptoms = Persistent delusions | Persistent hallucinations | Disorganised thinking (formal thought disorder) | Experiences of influence, passivity or control ~~ Negative symptoms :: They count towards the diagnosis, but they are not in the core group | Grossly disorganised behaviour :: It counts towards the diagnosis, but it is not in the core group | Psychomotor disturbance (catatonia) :: It counts towards the diagnosis, but it is not in the core group
> At least one of these four must be present.

S: Schneider's first-rank symptoms = Audible thoughts (thought echo) | Voices arguing or discussing | Voices giving a running commentary | Somatic passivity | Thought insertion | Thought withdrawal | Thought broadcasting | Delusional perception | Made feelings, impulses or actions ~~ Visual hallucinations :: Not a first-rank symptom | Persecutory delusions :: Not a first-rank symptom; delusional perception is | Negative symptoms :: Not a first-rank symptom | Second-person command voices :: Not first-rank; the voices are third-person (arguing or commenting)
> Group them: 3 auditory hallucinations, 3 thought alienations (insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting), passivity (made feelings, impulses, actions, somatic), and delusional perception.

Q: Which antipsychotic is uniquely proven to reduce suicide in schizophrenia? = Clozapine | Olanzapine :: Olanzapine has strong metabolic effects but no unique anti-suicide licence | Haloperidol :: First-generation antipsychotic with high EPS; does not reduce suicide risk | Risperidone :: High EPS and prolactin; does not share clozapine's anti-suicide evidence
> Clozapine is the only antipsychotic with FDA and international approval for reducing suicidal behaviour in schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder.

## Clozapine Clinic | OSCE: Clozapine Initiation & Blood Monitoring
> CANDIDATE BRIEF: A 28-year-old patient with treatment-resistant schizophrenia is referred after failing olanzapine and risperidone. Counsel the patient and their sister, explain the CPMS monitoring traffic lights, outline baseline tests, and take informed consent.
> CRITICAL FAIL: Failing to explain that fever or sore throat requires an immediate emergency hospital check; or combining clozapine with carbamazepine.

L: Clozapine Counselling Protocol (OSCE) = 1. Validate distress over past medication failures and define treatment resistance | 2. Explain Clozapine's superior efficacy and proven suicide-reduction evidence | 3. Explain mandatory blood monitoring: Weekly for 18 weeks, 2-weekly to 1 year, then monthly | 4. Explain the Traffic Light system: Green (continue), Amber (re-test 2x/wk), Red (stop immediately) | 5. Warn about emergency red flags: Fever, sore throat, severe constipation, and chest pain | 6. ## U-Block | Substance dependence (ICD-11)
> Walk the ICD-11 pathway for every substance, and show whether each requirement is present or absent. Don't force the diagnosis.
> - Is there a single episode of harmful use, or a harmful pattern of use (episodic or continuous) causing damage to health?
> - Dependence: at least 2 of 3 central features, usually over 12 months (or 3 months if use is continuous): impaired control; increasing precedence over other interests; physiological features (tolerance, withdrawal, use to relieve withdrawal).
> - Then add what else is present: intoxication, withdrawal, substance-induced disorders.
> Codes: 6C40 alcohol, 6C41 cannabis, 6C43 opioids, 6C44 sedatives, 6C45 cocaine, 6C46 stimulants. Suffixes: .0 episode of harmful use, .1 harmful pattern, .2 dependence, .3 intoxication, .4 withdrawal.

> In the long case, say each step out loud: "Impaired control: present, because... Physiological features: absent, because..."

S: ICD-11 dependence: the 3 central features = Impaired control over use | Increasing precedence of use over other interests | Physiological features (tolerance, withdrawal) ~~ Legal problems :: Not an ICD-11 dependence feature | Harm to other people :: That relates to harmful use, not dependence | Use of an illegal drug :: Legality is not a criterion
> - Impaired control: onset, amount, circumstances, termination; often with craving.
> - Precedence: use takes priority over health, responsibilities and interests, and continues despite harm.
> - Physiological: tolerance, withdrawal, or repeated use to prevent or relieve withdrawal.

Q: Features needed for ICD-11 dependence = At least 2 of the 3 central features | All 3 features :: Only two are needed | Any 1 feature :: One is not enough; two are needed | 3 of 6 features :: That was the ICD-10 rule (3 of 6)
> Two of three, usually evident over 12 months.

Q: Usual time frame for ICD-11 dependence = 12 months, or 3 months if use is continuous (daily or almost daily) | 12 months, or 1 month if use is continuous :: Continuous use needs at least 3 months | 6 months, or 1 month if use is continuous :: The usual period is 12 months, or 3 months if continuous | 12 months in every case :: It can be shortened to 3 months if use is continuous

> Physiological features alone are not dependence (for example, tolerance to prescribed opioids for pain). You still need a second feature.

Q: ICD-11 harmful pattern of substance use means = Use that damages health, without dependence | Use that damages health, with features of dependence :: If dependence is present, dependence is the diagnosis | A single episode of use that damages health :: That is an episode of harmful use, not a pattern | Use that causes social problems only, with no damage to health :: A harmful pattern requires damage to physical or mental health

## Sports Field | Lithium & mood stabilisers
> Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index and is excreted unchanged by the kidneys. Anything that reduces renal clearance raises the level: dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, thiazides, renal impairment.
> Know the targets and timing, baseline tests, the monitoring schedule, toxicity signs and management, and long-term effects: hypothyroidism, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, CKD, hyperparathyroidism with high calcium, weight gain, tremor.
> Other stabilisers: valproate (highly teratogenic), carbamazepine (enzyme inducer, hyponatraemia, Stevens-Johnson), lamotrigine (slow titration for rash).

Q: Usual maintenance lithium level = 0.6-1.0 mmol/L | 0.4-0.6 mmol/L :: Subtherapeutic for most people | 1.0-1.5 mmol/L :: Above the usual range and close to toxicity | 1.5-2.0 mmol/L :: That is the toxic range

Q: Lithium level is taken how long after the last dose? = 12 hours | 2 hours :: Near the peak, so the level would read falsely high | 24 hours :: Not standard; levels are standardised at 12 hours | 6 hours :: Not standard; levels are standardised at 12 hours

Q: Lithium toxicity usually appears above = 1.5 mmol/L | 1.0 mmol/L :: 1.0 is the top of the usual therapeutic range | 0.8 mmol/L :: 0.8 is therapeutic | 3.0 mmol/L :: Toxicity starts well before 3.0; that is severe, life-threatening

## West Courtyard | Antipsychotic emergencies | race

Q: Clozapine FBC monitoring (UK schedule) = Weekly for 18 weeks, fortnightly to 1 year, then monthly | Weekly for 6 weeks, fortnightly to 1 year, then monthly :: Weekly lasts 18 weeks | Weekly for 18 weeks, then monthly :: Fortnightly checks continue to 1 year before going monthly | Fortnightly for 18 weeks, then monthly :: The first 18 weeks are WEEKLY
> Results follow a traffic-light system (green, amber, red). A red result means stop clozapine.

Q: Clozapine myocarditis risk is highest = In the first 2 months | In the first 6 months :: Most cases are in the first 2 months | After the first year :: Myocarditis is an early complication | Only above 600 mg/day :: It is not mainly dose-related
> Monitor pulse, temperature, BP, troponin and CRP in the early weeks. Persistent tachycardia, fever, chest pain or breathlessness means stop and assess.

S: Serious clozapine adverse effects = Agranulocytosis | Myocarditis or cardiomyopathy | Seizures | Severe constipation (ileus) | Hypersalivation | Metabolic syndrome ~~ Raised prolactin :: Clozapine is prolactin-sparing | Tardive dyskinesia :: Clozapine has a very low risk | Hypothyroidism :: That is linked to lithium
> Constipation from clozapine can be fatal (ileus), so always ask about bowels and prescribe laxatives early.

L: Order of onset of extrapyramidal side effects = Acute dystonia (hours to days) | Akathisia (days to weeks) | Parkinsonism (weeks) | Tardive dyskinesia (months to years) ~~ NMS (always first) :: NMS is not an EPS stage; it is a separate emergency | Serotonin syndrome :: Not an antipsychotic EPS | Chorea in Huntington's :: Not drug-induced
> Rule of 4s: about 4 hours for acute dystonia, 4 days for akathisia, 4 weeks for parkinsonism, 4 months for tardive dyskinesia.

Q: Treatment of acute dystonia = IM or IV anticholinergic (e.g. procyclidine, biperiden) | IM benzodiazepine first line :: An anticholinergic is first line | Oral propranolol :: Propranolol is used for akathisia | Stop the antipsychotic and wait for it to pass :: Acute dystonia is distressing and can affect the airway; treat it promptly
> Acute dystonia includes oculogyric crisis, torticollis and laryngeal dystonia (an airway emergency). It is commoner in young men on high-potency drugs.

L: Acute dystonia management = Recognise the dystonia and reassure | IM or IV anticholinergic (e.g. procyclidine) | Repeat the dose if needed | Short oral anticholinergic course | Review the antipsychotic dose or choice ~~ Give more haloperidol :: The antipsychotic caused it | Propranolol first :: Propranolol is for akathisia | Wait and watch only :: It is distressing and can affect the airway; treat it
> IM or IV anticholinergics usually work within minutes.

Q: First steps for akathisia = Reduce the antipsychotic dose; consider propranolol | Add procyclidine as first line :: Anticholinergics help little in akathisia | Increase the antipsychotic dose :: That makes akathisia worse | Add a second antipsychotic :: That adds to the problem
> Akathisia (inner restlessness, pacing) is often mistaken for agitation or psychosis and wrongly treated with more antipsychotic. It is linked to suicide risk.

Q: Which drug class worsens tardive dyskinesia? = Anticholinergics (e.g. procyclidine) | VMAT2 inhibitors (e.g. valbenazine) :: These TREAT tardive dyskinesia | Clozapine :: Clozapine has a very low risk and may help | Beta blockers :: Not known to worsen it
> Management of TD: review the need for antipsychotics, consider clozapine or quetiapine, stop anticholinergics, and consider a VMAT2 inhibitor.

## Long Red Ward | Delirium & dementia
> Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, with cognitive change, caused by a medical condition, a substance, or withdrawal.
> - Subtypes: hyperactive, hypoactive (most often missed) and mixed.
> Dementia is a chronic, progressive decline in two or more cognitive domains, enough to impair independence. Delirium on top of dementia is common.
> Know each dementia by its signature: Alzheimer's (early memory loss), vascular (stepwise decline), Lewy body (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism), frontotemporal (behaviour and language first).

Q: Hallmark of delirium = Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness | Acute, stable impairment of memory with clear consciousness :: Delirium fluctuates, and attention and awareness are impaired | Gradual disturbance of attention over months :: Delirium develops over hours to days | Acute psychosis in clear consciousness :: Clear consciousness points away from delirium
> Inattention is the core sign. Test it with months of the year backwards or digit span.

S: CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) features = Acute onset and fluctuating course | Inattention | Disorganised thinking | Altered level of consciousness ~~ Visual hallucinations :: Common in delirium but not a CAM feature | Memory loss over months :: That suggests dementia | Low mood :: Not a CAM feature
> CAM-positive = feature 1 + feature 2 + either feature 3 or feature 4.

Q: Bedside delirium screening tool = CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) | AMTS :: The Abbreviated Mental Test is a cognitive screen, not delirium-specific | MoCA :: The MoCA screens cognition, not delirium | GCS :: The Glasgow Coma Scale rates level of consciousness only
> The 4AT is another quick tool: Alertness, AMT4, Attention (months backwards), and acute change.

Q: Delirium subtype most often missed = Hypoactive | Hyperactive :: Agitation makes this one obvious | Mixed :: Fluctuating agitation usually gets noticed | Catatonic :: Not a standard delirium subtype
> A quiet, withdrawn older patient may be labelled depressed. Hypoactive delirium has the worse outcomes.

Q: Commonest cause of dementia = Alzheimer's disease | Vascular dementia :: Second commonest | Lewy body dementia :: Less common than Alzheimer's | Frontotemporal dementia :: More common in younger-onset dementia, but not overall
> Alzheimer's pathology: amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. It starts in the medial temporal lobe (hippocampus), so early memory loss is typical.

S: The 4 As of Alzheimer's disease = Amnesia | Aphasia | Agnosia | Apraxia ~~ Ataxia :: Not one of the 4 As | Alogia :: A negative symptom of schizophrenia | Anhedonia :: A depressive symptom | Akathisia :: An antipsychotic side effect
> Amnesia (memory), aphasia (language), agnosia (recognition), apraxia (skilled movement).

S: Core features of dementia with Lewy bodies = Fluctuating cognition | Recurrent visual hallucinations | REM sleep behaviour disorder | Parkinsonism ~~ Early prominent memory loss :: Typical of Alzheimer's disease | Stepwise decline :: Typical of vascular dementia | Chorea :: Typical of Huntington's disease | Early disinhibition :: Typical of frontotemporal dementia
> McKeith 2017 consensus: two or more core features = probable DLB.

Q: Severe antipsychotic sensitivity is a red flag for = Dementia with Lewy bodies | Alzheimer's disease :: Antipsychotics carry risk, but severe sensitivity is the DLB hallmark | Vascular dementia :: Not the classic association | Delirium tremens :: DTs is treated with benzodiazepines; not this red flag
> Up to half of DLB patients react badly to antipsychotics (rigidity, falls, NMS-like reactions). Avoid typical antipsychotics.

Q: Pick's disease is a form of = Frontotemporal dementia | Vascular dementia :: Vascular dementia is caused by cerebrovascular disease | Prion disease :: Prion disease is CJD | Lewy body dementia :: Lewy bodies are a different pathology
> FTD presents earlier (often before 65), with behaviour change (disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy) or language problems, while memory is relatively spared at first.

Q: Stepwise decline with focal signs suggests = Vascular dementia | Alzheimer's disease :: Alzheimer's shows a gradual, steady decline | Lewy body dementia :: DLB fluctuates, with hallucinations and parkinsonism | Huntington's disease :: Huntington's shows chorea and is autosomal dominant
> Look for vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, AF) and earlier slowing and executive problems.

S: Normal pressure hydrocephalus triad = Gait disturbance | Urinary incontinence | Cognitive decline ~~ Visual hallucinations :: Not part of the triad | Seizures :: Not part of the triad | Chorea :: Not part of the triad
> "Wet, wobbly and wacky". A potentially reversible cause of dementia: think of it when gait problems come FIRST.

L: Delirium management = Identify and treat the underlying cause | Review and stop offending drugs | Non-drug measures: reorientation, lighting, family, sleep | Short-term low-dose antipsychotic only if distressed or a risk | Follow up cognition after recovery ~~ Start an antipsychotic first :: Treat the cause first; drugs are a last resort | Routine benzodiazepines :: They can worsen delirium (except alcohol withdrawal) | Physical restraint :: Avoid; it worsens agitation and injury risk
> Common causes: infection, drugs (anticholinergics, opioids, sedatives), metabolic problems, hypoxia, urinary retention, constipation, pain, withdrawal.

## Round Huts | Emergency protocols | race
> Emergencies are examined as sequences. The same principles run through all of them: safety first, the least restrictive option, treat the medical cause, monitor, document, and debrief.

L: Rapid tranquillisation steps = Verbal de-escalation | Offer oral medication | IM medication (e.g. lorazepam, or haloperidol with promethazine) | Monitor vital signs afterwards | Debrief patient and staff ~~ Physical restraint first :: Use the least restrictive option first | Electroconvulsive therapy :: Not part of rapid tranquillisation | IV haloperidol first :: Start with de-escalation, then oral, then IM
> If there is no ECG, or there is cardiac disease or a long QT, use IM lorazepam rather than haloperidol.

Q: Vital signs after IM rapid tranquillisation = At least hourly until no concerns; every 15 minutes if high dose, asleep or physically unwell | Every 15 minutes for the first hour, then stop if calm :: Continue at least hourly until there are no concerns | Hourly for the first hour only :: Monitoring continues until there are no concerns | Every 4 hours :: Too infrequent
> Monitor pulse, BP, respiratory rate, temperature, hydration and level of consciousness. A sleeping patient needs closer, not looser, observation.

Q: Avoid haloperidol for rapid tranquillisation when = There is no ECG, or there is cardiac disease or a prolonged QT | The patient is very agitated :: Agitation is the indication | The patient has taken antipsychotics before :: Previous antipsychotic exposure makes haloperidol MORE suitable | The patient has asthma :: Not a contraindication to haloperidol
> Haloperidol prolongs QT. Promethazine is added to reduce dystonia and add sedation.
> NICE also advises IM lorazepam if there is not enough information to choose, or if the person has never taken an antipsychotic.

Q: Drug to reverse benzodiazepine respiratory depression = Flumazenil | Naloxone :: Naloxone reverses opioids | Procyclidine :: Procyclidine treats acute dystonia | Atropine :: Atropine treats bradycardia and organophosphate poisoning
> Flumazenil can trigger seizures in people dependent on benzodiazepines or after mixed overdoses. Give it carefully, with airway support first.

L: NMS management = Stop the antipsychotic | Supportive care: cooling and IV fluids | Monitor CK, renal function and vital signs | Transfer to medical or ICU care | Consider benzodiazepine, bromocriptine or dantrolene | Wait at least 2 weeks before a cautious rechallenge ~~ Increase the antipsychotic dose :: The antipsychotic is the cause; stop it | Add a second antipsychotic :: Makes NMS worse | Give IM haloperidol :: An antipsychotic would worsen NMS
> On rechallenge, use a low-potency or atypical drug, start low, go slow, and monitor temperature and CK.

L: Alcohol detox essentials = Assess severity (CIWA-Ar) | Give thiamine before glucose | Benzodiazepine reducing regimen (e.g. chlordiazepoxide) | Watch for seizures and delirium | Plan relapse prevention ~~ Give glucose before thiamine :: Wrong order; thiamine first to avoid Wernicke's | Start disulfiram during acute withdrawal :: Disulfiram is for relapse prevention after detox, never during withdrawal | Stop all fluids :: No role
> Relapse prevention options: acamprosate (reduces craving), naltrexone (reduces the reward), disulfiram (aversive). Add psychosocial support and AA.

Q: Why give thiamine before glucose? = To avoid precipitating Wernicke's encephalopathy | To prevent refeeding syndrome :: Thiamine is part of refeeding care, but the reason here is Wernicke's | To prevent hypoglycaemia :: Glucose prevents hypoglycaemia; thiamine is about Wernicke's | To prevent Korsakoff syndrome only :: It prevents Wernicke's encephalopathy, which can then lead to Korsakoff's
> Glucose metabolism uses up thiamine. In a depleted drinker, glucose alone can tip them into Wernicke's.

L: Lithium toxicity management = Stop lithium | Check lithium level, U&E and ECG | IV fluids and correct electrolytes | Consider haemodialysis if severe ~~ Give NSAIDs for pain :: NSAIDs RAISE lithium levels | Add a thiazide diuretic :: Thiazides RAISE lithium levels | Give the next lithium dose :: Stop lithium first
> Activated charcoal does NOT bind lithium. Consider haemodialysis for severe toxicity, a very high level, or renal failure.

L: ECT procedure = Informed consent | Pre-ECT work-up (examination, bloods, ECG, anaesthetic review) | Nil by mouth | General anaesthetic and muscle relaxant | Deliver the electrical stimulus | Monitor the seizure (EEG and motor) | Recovery observation ~~ Give lithium before ECT :: Lithium with ECT raises the risk of prolonged confusion | Benzodiazepine just before :: Raises the seizure threshold | Skip consent :: Consent (or the legal process) is always required
> Indications: severe depression (especially psychotic, or with refusal of food and fluids), catatonia, treatment-resistant mania.
> Common side effects: headache, muscle aches, transient confusion and memory impairment (mostly around the treatment period).

Q: Commonest cognitive side effect of ECT = Short-term memory impairment around the treatment period | Permanent loss of all memories from before ECT :: Not typical; effects are mostly around the treatment period | Impaired procedural memory (skills) :: Procedural memory is usually preserved | Lasting aphasia :: Not an ECT effect
> Right unilateral electrode placement causes less memory impairment than bilateral, but may be slightly less effective.

## South Ward | OSCE: history taking & risk
> OSCE stations are marked against a checklist. Examiners reward structure, and they reward you for naming each domain out loud.
> - Open every station the same way: introduce yourself, confirm the patient's name, explain the task, get consent, and mention confidentiality and its limits.
> - Start with open questions, then funnel down to closed ones. Summarise back regularly. Close with a summary, the plan, and "Is there anything I haven't asked that you think is important?"
> - Global marks come from rapport, empathy statements, avoiding jargon, and keeping to time. Practise each checklist until you can produce the next domain without prompting.

L: OSCE: suicide risk assessment after an overdose = Introduce, consent, confidentiality and its limits | Open question: tell me what happened | Details of the act: method, planning, timing, precautions against discovery, final acts | Intent then: did you expect to die? Feelings about surviving | Current suicidal ideas, plans and access to means | Past self-harm or suicide attempts | Psychiatric symptoms now: depression, psychosis, substance use | Risk factors: social, physical illness, family history, recent loss | Protective factors: family, faith, children, future plans | Summarise, state the risk, safety plan and follow-up ~~ Promise to keep everything secret :: Never promise absolute confidentiality; risk to life overrides it | Avoid asking directly about suicide :: Asking directly does not increase risk and is expected by examiners | Discharge once medically fit :: Every self-harm presentation needs a psychosocial assessment first
> Examiners look for:
> - Direct questions: "Are you having thoughts of ending your life?"
> - High-intent markers: planning, isolation, precautions against discovery, a final act (note, will, goodbyes), a violent or lethal method, and regret at surviving.
> - Access to means (stored medicines, pesticides, firearms) and protective factors.
> - A clear risk statement at the end: low, moderate or high, with reasons, plus a safety plan.

L: OSCE: alcohol history = Introduce, consent, non-judgemental opening | Typical drinking day: what, how much, when, where, with whom | Quantity in standard drinks or units, and how it has changed | Dependence features: impaired control, precedence, tolerance and withdrawal | Withdrawal complications: seizures, delirium tremens, hallucinations | Consequences: physical, psychological, social, occupational, legal, financial | Previous treatment and longest period of abstinence | Other substances and smoking | Motivation and stage of change | Summarise and agree a plan ~~ Start with "Are you an alcoholic?" :: Labelling is confrontational and loses rapport | Skip the typical day :: The typical-day walk-through is the most reliable way to quantify drinking | Moralise about drinking :: Stay non-judgemental; motivational style scores higher
> Examiners look for:
> - The "typical day" technique and a quantity estimate.
> - Explicit ICD-11 dependence features (impaired control, precedence, physiological features) and withdrawal history, especially seizures and DTs, because they change detox safety.
> - Motivation assessed with the stages of change.
> Ask about drink-driving and about children at home: safeguarding.

L: OSCE: first-episode psychosis history = Introduce and consent | Presenting complaint and timeline (onset, duration, course) | Hallucinations: modality, person (2nd or 3rd), content, commands | Delusions: content, conviction, evidence the person gives | Passivity and thought alienation (insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting) | Negative symptoms | Mood symptoms (to separate from mania or psychotic depression) | Substance use, especially cannabis and stimulants | Risk: to self, to others, from others; command hallucinations | Insight | Past psychiatric, medical, family and personal history | Summarise and plan ~~ Argue with the delusion :: Never challenge or collude; explore it neutrally | Skip substance use :: Substances are a key cause and trigger of psychosis | Ask only yes/no questions :: Open questions first, then specific screens
> Examiners look for:
> - Screening each first-rank symptom with clear lay wording: "Do you ever hear your thoughts spoken aloud?" "Do you feel your thoughts are being taken out of your head?"
> - Command hallucinations and violence risk.
> - Cannabis.
> - Insight in its three parts.
> Close with differentials: schizophrenia, substance-induced psychosis, psychotic mood disorder, organic cause.

L: OSCE: collateral history from a relative = Introduce, explain your role, confirm the patient's consent or the reason to proceed without it | What changes have you noticed, and when did they start? | Timeline of the current episode | Specific behaviours: sleep, eating, self-care, speech, odd beliefs, aggression | Risk observed: self-harm, threats, violence, wandering, vulnerability | Substance use as they have seen it | Premorbid personality: what was he or she like before? | Past episodes and treatment response | Family history and social circumstances | Carer strain and support needs | Summarise and thank them ~~ Share all medical details freely :: Respect confidentiality; listen more than you disclose without consent | Skip premorbid personality :: Collateral is the best source of premorbid personality | Ignore carer strain :: Examiners reward asking how the carer is coping
> Collateral history adds what the patient cannot report: the true timeline, premorbid personality, risk behaviours, and the effect on the family. You can always listen to a relative even without consent. What you share depends on consent.

Q: In an OSCE risk assessment, asking directly about suicidal thoughts = Does not increase risk and is expected | Slightly increases risk but is still required :: Asking directly does NOT increase risk | Should be left to the end of the interview :: Ask when the history leads there, not as an afterthought | Is only needed if the patient is depressed :: Ask in every psychiatric assessment
> Asking directly and calmly is protective. It shows you take the person seriously and often brings relief.

Q: Which is a high-intent feature of a suicide attempt? = Taking precautions against being discovered | Taking an overdose in front of family :: Acting where help is likely suggests lower intent | Calling a friend soon afterwards :: Seeking help suggests lower intent | An impulsive overdose after an argument :: Impulsive acts can still be dangerous, but planning and concealment mark higher intent
> High-intent markers: planning, choosing a time when no one will find you, precautions against discovery, final acts (a note or will), believing the method would be lethal, and regret at surviving.

## Courtyard Block | OSCE: explaining treatment
> Explanation stations are marked on structure and on checking understanding. Use the same frame every time:
> - Find out what they know and what they want to know (avoid lecturing).
> - Give information in small chunks, in plain language. Check understanding with teach-back ("Can you tell me in your own words…?").
> - Cover benefits, risks, alternatives, and what happens if they do nothing.
> - Invite questions, offer a leaflet, and agree a follow-up.

L: OSCE: explaining lithium to a patient = Introduce, check what they already know and want to know | What lithium is and why it is recommended (prevents relapse, reduces suicide risk) | How to take it and how long before it works | Blood tests: level 12 hours after a dose, then regular checks | Common side effects: thirst, passing more urine, fine tremor, weight gain, stomach upset | Toxicity warning signs: vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor, unsteadiness, confusion: stop and seek help | Avoid dehydration; avoid NSAIDs; tell every doctor you take lithium | Pregnancy and contraception | Long-term monitoring of kidneys, thyroid and calcium | Check understanding, give a leaflet and lithium card, invite questions ~~ Say it has no side effects :: Inaccurate and loses marks for honesty | Skip toxicity warning signs :: Toxicity counselling is the most heavily weighted point | Tell them to stop suddenly if they feel well :: Stopping abruptly raises the risk of rebound mania
> Examiners look for: a clear toxicity safety-net, the dehydration and NSAID warnings, the 12-hour blood level, pregnancy counselling, and teach-back.
> Common fail: listing side effects without explaining what to DO about them.

L: OSCE: explaining clozapine = Check understanding: why clozapine (when 2 antipsychotics have not worked) | Benefits: often works when others have not, reduces suicide risk | Blood monitoring: weekly for 18 weeks, then fortnightly, then monthly, because of low white cells | Report infection signs urgently: sore throat, fever | Common side effects: drowsiness, drooling, constipation, weight gain, dizziness | Constipation can be serious: report it, take laxatives | Heart warning signs early on: chest pain, breathlessness, fast heartbeat | Smoking changes clozapine levels: tell us if you stop or start | Missed doses for 48 hours or more need re-titration | Check understanding and invite questions ~~ Blood tests are optional :: Monitoring is mandatory to continue clozapine | Constipation is harmless :: Clozapine constipation can cause fatal bowel obstruction | Smoking makes no difference :: Smoking induces CYP1A2; stopping smoking can raise clozapine levels sharply
> Examiners look for: the reason for monitoring (neutropenia), sore throat or fever as an emergency, constipation, myocarditis warning signs, the smoking interaction, and what to do about missed doses.

L: OSCE: explaining ECT = Check understanding and concerns | What ECT is: a brief, controlled seizure under general anaesthetic | Why it is recommended (severe or psychotic depression, catatonia, not eating or drinking) | What happens: fasting, anaesthetic, muscle relaxant, monitoring, recovery | How many: usually twice weekly, often 6 to 12 treatments | Benefits: often works faster than medication in severe illness | Side effects: headache, muscle aches, confusion afterwards, memory problems | Alternatives and the right to refuse or withdraw consent at any time | Address myths, check understanding, invite questions ~~ Say it is painful and done awake :: ECT is given under general anaesthetic with a muscle relaxant | Promise no memory effects :: Memory problems can occur and must be discussed honestly | Say consent cannot be withdrawn :: Consent can be withdrawn at any time
> Myths to address: it is not a punishment, they will be asleep, it does not change personality, and the amount of electricity is carefully controlled.

L: OSCE: explaining a diagnosis of schizophrenia to a relative = Check consent and what they know | Explain the illness simply: changes in thinking and perception | Common symptoms they may see | Causes: a mix of genes, brain chemistry, stress and drugs like cannabis (not bad parenting or a curse) | Treatment: medication, talking therapies, family support, rehabilitation | Outlook: many recover well, and relapse can be prevented | Early warning signs of relapse | How the family can help: low expressed emotion, adherence support | Support services and carer support | Check understanding and invite questions ~~ Blame the family :: Blaming causes harm and is factually wrong | Say it is untreatable :: Outcomes improve with treatment; give realistic hope | Confirm it is a spiritual attack :: Respect beliefs, but explain the medical model clearly
> Cultural sensitivity scores marks: acknowledge the family's beliefs (spiritual or traditional explanations), then explain the medical view without dismissing them. High expressed emotion (criticism, hostility, over-involvement) predicts relapse, so family psychoeducation is evidence-based.

Q: Best way to check understanding in an explanation station = Ask the patient to explain it back in their own words (teach-back) | Ask "Do you have any questions?" :: This invites questions but does not check understanding | Ask "Do you understand?" :: A closed question; patients usually just say yes | Summarise the key points for them yourself :: Your summary does not show what THEY understood
> Teach-back is the single most rewarded technique in explanation stations.

## SE Clinic | OSCE: examination, capacity & communication
> Practical stations mix examination, legal reasoning and communication. Say what you are doing as you do it, so the examiner can tick it.

L: OSCE: examining for extrapyramidal side effects = Introduce, consent, explain the examination | Observe at rest: tremor, abnormal movements, posture, facial expression | Tone at wrist and elbow: cogwheel or lead-pipe rigidity (use an activation manoeuvre) | Bradykinesia: finger tapping, hand opening | Gait: arm swing, shuffling, turning | Akathisia: watch for restlessness, ask about inner restlessness | Tardive dyskinesia: orofacial movements, tongue, limbs, trunk (AIMS style) | Summarise findings, offer temperature and observations if NMS is suspected ~~ Skip asking about inner restlessness :: Akathisia is partly subjective; you must ask | Test reflexes only :: Reflexes do not assess EPS | Forget to observe the tongue :: Tongue and orofacial movements are the commonest signs of tardive dyskinesia
> Name each sign as you find it. Rating scales: Simpson-Angus (parkinsonism), Barnes (akathisia), AIMS (tardive dyskinesia).

L: OSCE: assessing capacity to refuse treatment = Introduce and explain the purpose | Confirm the specific decision and give the relevant information | Can they understand the information? | Can they retain it long enough to decide? | Can they use or weigh it, including consequences of refusing? | Can they communicate a decision? | Conclude: has or lacks capacity for THIS decision, now | Document and plan (best interests if capacity is lacking) ~~ Assume no capacity because of a mental illness :: Capacity is presumed; diagnosis alone does not remove it | Decide based on whether you agree with the choice :: An unwise decision does not mean lack of capacity | Assess capacity for all decisions at once :: Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific
> The four-part functional test (taught from the UK Mental Capacity Act 2005; similar common-law principles apply elsewhere): understand, retain, use or weigh, communicate. Failing any one means lacking capacity for that decision.
> Key principles: presume capacity, support decision-making, an unwise decision is not incapacity, and assess decision by decision.

L: OSCE: dealing with an angry relative = Acknowledge the anger and introduce yourself calmly | Move to a private place and sit down | Listen without interrupting | Empathise and name the emotion | Clarify the specific concerns | Apologise for their experience without blaming colleagues | Explain what you can, within confidentiality | Agree a plan and timeline | Offer the complaints route and follow-up ~~ Match their tone :: Escalation loses marks; stay calm | Blame a colleague :: Never criticise colleagues; take ownership | Refuse to discuss anything :: Listen and share what consent allows
> Examiners score de-escalation, empathy statements ("I can see how upsetting this has been"), honesty, and a concrete plan.

Q: An unwise decision by a patient means = Nothing on its own about capacity | Strong evidence that they lack capacity :: An unwise decision is not, by itself, evidence of incapacity | They must be assessed under mental health law :: Not automatically; mental health law has its own criteria | Their next of kin decides for them :: Relatives cannot simply override a capacitous adult
> Capacity is about the decision-making process, not the outcome you would choose.

Q: The four abilities in a capacity assessment = Understand, retain, use or weigh, communicate | Understand, remember, agree, communicate :: Agreeing is not part of the test; it is retain and use or weigh | Understand, retain, use or weigh, consent :: The fourth is COMMUNICATE the decision | Understand, retain, use or weigh, comply :: The fourth is COMMUNICATE the decision
> Think "URWC" (Understand, Retain, Weigh, Communicate).
